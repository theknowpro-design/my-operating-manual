My role spans three areas: (1) technical strategy—setting the direction for our platform architecture, (2) people leadership—hiring, mentoring, and unblocking my team, and (3) execution—owning the delivery of our most critical OKRs.

I think of myself as a bridge between business and engineering. I'm comfortable in technical deep dives, but I also spend significant time in product planning, customer conversations, and leadership meetings. I bring a systems-thinking mindset to everything—I'm always asking, "How does this decision affect the whole system?"

I've been in startups for 10 years, and I've seen both explosive growth and near-death experiences. That history shapes how I approach trade-offs: I'm comfortable with ambiguity early on, but once we commit to a direction, I'm very rigorous about execution. Also, I'm based in San Francisco but my team is distributed across 4 time zones, so async clarity is non-negotiable for me.

## PHASE 2 — VALUES & PRINCIPLES

### Core Values
1. **Clarity over speed.** I will slow down meetings, push back on vague requirements, and ask "stupid questions" to ensure everyone understands the actual problem. Ambiguous direction leads to wasted work, and I'd rather spend 30 minutes aligning on the problem than waste two weeks building the wrong thing.

2. **Technical excellence with pragmatism.** I care deeply about code quality, architecture, and the long-term health of our systems. But I'm not a perfectionist. If a scrappy solution buys us time to learn the actual customer need, I'm fine with that. I just want to know we're making an intentional trade-off.

3. **Trust-based autonomy.** I hire strong people and give them clear context, then I get out of the way. Micromanagement is my kryptonite. I trust my team to make good decisions, and I don't second-guess them unless there's a real risk to the system.

4. **Radical honesty.** I will give you direct feedback, even if it's uncomfortable. I expect the same in return. Sugarcoating problems doesn't help anyone; it just delays fixing them.

5. **People over politics.** I optimize for team health, individual growth, and psychological safety. I won't play politics or throw someone under the bus to make myself look good.

### Non-Negotiable Value
**Clarity.** I will push back on shipping vague requirements, even if it delays a deadline by a sprint. I'll walk into uncomfortable conversations to align on what we're actually building. I've learned the hard way that unclear direction is the most expensive thing a team can work with—it leads to rework, frustration, and burnout. So I protect clarity ruthlessly.

## PHASE 3 — STRENGTHS & SUPERPOWERS

### Greatest Strengths
1. **Systems thinking.** I naturally see how different parts of a system connect. I can zoom in and out between tactical details and strategic implications. This helps me spot bottlenecks, predict failure modes, and spot opportunities for leverage.

2. **Technical depth.** I'm still hands-on enough to understand the nuances of our architecture. I code maybe 10% of the time now, but it keeps me grounded. I can have credible conversations with my team about trade-offs between different technical approaches.

3. **Async clarity.** I'm disciplined about writing. I write detailed design docs, clear Slack summaries, and structured decision notes. People often tell me my docs are unusually clear for a technical person.

4. **Mentorship.** I genuinely enjoy helping junior engineers grow. I ask good questions, I give them hard problems with support, and I celebrate their wins. Several engineers on my team credit me with major career moves.

5. **Crisis management.** When things break, I stay calm and focus the team on the critical path. I'm good at triaging severity, isolating scope, and communicating status to anxious stakeholders.

### Recent Example
Last month, we had a customer data loss incident. One table got corrupted during a migration. My systems-thinking approach kicked in: I immediately asked, "How many customers affected? What's the recovery time?" instead of jumping to blame. Then I mapped out three parallel workstreams: (1) incident response, (2) customer communication, (3) post-mortem and process change.

My async clarity helped too. I wrote a status doc every 30 minutes for 6 hours, with clear updates, next steps, and confidence levels. It kept everyone aligned without needing synchronous meetings that would've slowed us down.

### What I Don't Own
- **Politics and office navigation.** I'm not good at playing politics or building visibility with executives. I'm fine being behind-the-scenes if the work is good.
- **Spreadsheet management and administrative overhead.** I can do it, but it drains me. I'd rather delegate this to someone who enjoys organization.
- **Consensus-building on every decision.** I value input, but I won't spend weeks getting perfect agreement. I'll make a decision, explain the reasoning, and move forward.
- **Social event planning or team morale activities.** My team needs psychological safety and clear expectations, not happy hours. (Though I'll show up if you organize them!)

## PHASE 4 — GROWTH EDGES

### Active Growth Areas
1. **Delegation and letting go.** I've become better at this, but I still default to rolling up my sleeves when I see inefficiency. I need to let my team own more and resist the urge to optimize every process myself.

2. **Executive presence and visibility.** I'm naturally quiet and heads-down. I'm working on showing up more in company-wide forums, sharing wins, and building visibility with executives. Not because I care about politics, but because it helps my team get resources.

3. **Patience with slower decision-makers.** I move fast and expect others to keep up. I'm working on slowing down, giving more context, and being genuinely curious about why someone else's pace is different.

4. **Saying no strategically.** I tend to say yes to too many initiatives because I see how they connect to the bigger picture. I need to be more ruthless about scope.

### Early Warning Signs
- **Delegation edge**: I notice myself rewriting someone's design doc or jumping into a sprint planning meeting uninvited. That's a sign I'm not trusting my team.
- **Visibility edge**: I go a month without sharing a major win in all-hands or with my skip-level manager. I feel invisible and then resent not getting credit.
- **Patience edge**: I feel frustrated in a meeting where the group is taking too long to decide. I want to say "We're overthinking this" but I stay quiet and stew.
- **Scope edge**: I'm in back-to-back meetings about different initiatives. I feel stretched and exhausted by Friday.

## PHASE 5 — COMMUNICATION STYLE

### How I Communicate Best
I prefer **async, written communication** over meetings. I'll write a detailed Slack thread, a design doc, or a structured email. If you give me 10 minutes, I'll write something clear and thoughtful that you can parse in 5 minutes. Synchronous meetings feel slower to me—too much context-switching, not enough time for deep thinking.

That said, I value a few synchronous moments: (1) **one-on-ones** with my direct reports (weekly, 30 min), (2) **design reviews** on critical decisions (can be async or sync, but needs depth), and (3) **urgent incidents** (obviously sync).

I'm direct and data-driven. I use examples, numbers, and clear logic. I don't do a lot of small talk, and I can come across as blunt. I'm working on adding more warmth and context.

### Preferred Channels
- **Routine updates**: Slack thread or email (async, no response needed immediately).
- **Decisions that affect my team**: Slack message with a link to a design doc. I'll comment and iterate async.
- **Decisions that need my input**: Slack message with "FYI" or "Need your thoughts by EOD Thursday" (clear deadline).
- **Urgent issues**: Slack @mention or ping me on Slack (I check it ~every 15 min). If it's critical, a quick call.
- **Feedback**: One-on-one call preferred, but I can do async Slack if timing is tight.

### Common Misreadings
People often think I'm **angry or annoyed when I'm just being direct**. My default tone is blunt. When I ask tough questions in a meeting ("Why are we doing this?"), some people interpret it as criticism or hostility. I actually mean it as curiosity.

Also, people assume I **prefer email over Slack**, which makes me seem cold. The truth is I prefer async + written, but Slack is fine. I just don't do well with surprise synchronous meetings or being pinged expecting an immediate answer.

## PHASE 6 — WORKING STYLE & ENERGY

### Planning and Focus
I work in **two-week planning cycles**. Sunday evening, I map out the two big initiatives I'm focusing on (usually one technical, one people-related). I protect **Monday and Tuesday mornings** for deep work—no meetings. Afternoons are for 1-on-1s and tactical decisions.

Wednesdays and Thursdays are my "meeting days"—I batch all the meetings and sync work. Fridays are a mix of loose ends and reflection. Friday afternoon, I review the week and plan next week.

I'm an **introvert who gets energized by focused conversations** with people I trust, but I drain from noisy environments or back-to-back social interaction. After a day of meetings, I need quiet time (often a walk or some solo coding) to recharge.

### Key Rituals
1. **Coffee and a written plan.** I start every morning with a strong coffee and a handwritten plan for the day (3-5 things). Analog planning helps me focus better than digital.

2. **"Focus mornings" (Mon-Tue, 9-12):** Blocked on calendar, Slack off. If it's truly urgent, call me. Otherwise, I'll respond at noon.

3. **A quiet office space.** I work in a small room with a door. Open offices destroy my focus. Some teams have co-working norms; I literally can't work in those spaces.

4. **Walking one-on-ones.** I have my best conversations while walking. Moving helps me think and relax.

5. **Weekly retro on my own work.** Friday afternoon, I review: What did I plan vs. what I actually did? Where did I get derailed? It helps me calibrate better.

## PHASE 7 — DECISION MAKING

### Decision Framework
I use a **"reversible vs. irreversible" framework**.

**Irreversible decisions** (e.g., hiring someone, choosing a database technology, sunsetting a product) get high scrutiny. I'll ask hard questions, seek diverse input, run scenarios, and move slower. I want high confidence (70-80%) before committing.

**Reversible decisions** (e.g., trying a new process, running an experiment, changing a tool) I make faster. I'll check with 1-2 stakeholders, but I'll often just try it. If it doesn't work, we course-correct. This bias toward experimentation helps us move faster on low-stakes things.

I also use **data when available**, but I don't wait for perfect data. If we have 70% of the information and it points clearly in one direction, I'll move. I'd rather be 80% right and move than 100% right and be late.

### What Slows Me Down vs. Speeds Me Up
**Slows me down:**
- Unclear problem statements
- Too many stakeholders with conflicting opinions
- Lack of historical context (why did we decide this before?)
- Meetings where people aren't prepared

**Speeds me up:**
- Clear problem + context written down
- 2-3 trusted opinions (not 10)
- Authority to decide (vs. needing consensus)
- Tight deadlines (I work faster under constraint)

### Reversible vs. Irreversible Treatment
Reversible decisions get 30-60 minutes of thought. Irreversible decisions get 2-3 days of structured thinking, with input from stakeholders.

## PHASE 8 — FEEDBACK PREFERENCES

### Giving and Receiving Feedback
I give feedback **early and directly**. If I see something that isn't working, I'll bring it up in a 1-on-1 within a few days. I'm not interested in letting problems fester. I frame it as "I noticed X, and I want to help you succeed. Here's what I'd suggest..." but I'm direct about the issue.

I receive feedback well when it's **specific, data-backed, and framed as support**. "You're not strategic enough" is useless. "In the last three meetings, you focused on implementation details before we'd agreed on requirements. That slowed decisions. Next time, I'd suggest leading with 'What problem are we solving?'" is helpful.

I prefer feedback **one-on-one and in private**. Public feedback feels humiliating to me, even if it's positive. I'd rather hear wins in a quiet moment than have someone shout me out in a meeting.

### Cadence and Emotional Preferences
- **Frequency**: Monthly formal feedback in 1-on-1s. Quick, informal feedback anytime something happens (within days, not weeks later).
- **Tone**: Direct, kind, and growth-oriented. Assume good intent. Focus on behavior, not personality.
- **Setting**: Private, 1-on-1, no surprises.
- **Framing**: "Here's what I observed. Here's what I think it blocks. Here's how I'd approach it differently."

### Public vs. Private Feedback
- **Public**: Wins, milestone achievements, promotions, retirements. Celebrate those loudly.
- **Private**: Anything corrective, anything that touches on competence, anything that might embarrass someone. Always private.

## PHASE 9 — TRUST & COLLABORATION

### What Builds Trust
1. **Radical honesty.** Say what you actually think, even if it's uncomfortable. Don't hide behind pleasantries.
2. **Follow through.** Say you'll do something and do it. No surprises, no excuses.
3. **Intellectual humility.** Be willing to say "I was wrong" or "I don't know." Pretending to know everything destroys trust.
4. **Competence.** Do good work. Take ownership. Don't make excuses.
5. **Confidentiality.** Don't spread gossip or share private conversations. What I tell you in a 1-on-1 stays there.

### What Erodes Trust
1. **Vagueness.** Hiding bad news, being unclear about status, or dodging hard questions.
2. **Flakiness.** Saying yes and not delivering, canceling plans, or being consistently late.
3. **Politicking.** Saying one thing to my face and another behind my back. Throwing people under the bus.
4. **Incompetence without effort.** I forgive mistakes if you're trying hard and learning. I don't forgive apathy.
5. **Broken confidentiality.** If I find out you told someone something I told you in confidence, I'm done.

### Repairing Broken Trust
First, **own it immediately**. "I screwed up. Here's what I did wrong and what I'll do differently." Don't make excuses or blame circumstances.

Second, **show change through action**. Don't just apologize; prove over time that the behavior has changed. One apology isn't enough.

Third, **be patient with me rebuilding**. I don't snap back to trusting someone overnight. Trust is rebuilt slower than it's broken.

## PHASE 10 — BOUNDARIES & CONSTRAINTS

### Hard Boundaries
1. **No meetings before 10 AM.** I use 8-10 AM for deep work or my morning prep. A meeting during that window destroys my entire day.

2. **"Focus mornings" (Mon-Tue, 9-12):** Blocked on calendar, Slack off. If it's truly urgent, call me. Otherwise, I'll respond at noon.

3. **No Slack after 7 PM or before 8 AM.** I don't respond to messages outside work hours. If it's urgent, call my personal number (on my Slack profile). Otherwise, it waits until morning.

4. **One skip-level per week.** I do one meeting with my manager's manager, not three. If there are multiple priorities, they need to align on what matters most.

5. **No surprises in meetings.** If you want to discuss something sensitive or important, send me the topic beforehand. I think better with prep time.

6. **No more than 4 hours of meetings per day.** Beyond that, I'm cognitively fried. If a day has more than 4 hours scheduled, I'll push some meetings to other days.

### Acceptable Exceptions
- **True emergencies.** If the system is down and customers are affected, ignore all boundaries. Call/Slack me anytime.
- **Important customer calls.** If a high-value customer is on the line and needs my input, I'll join even if it's before 10 AM.
- **Leadership crises.** If my skip-level manager says "We need you now," I'll show up.
- **Hiring.** If we're in final rounds with a candidate, I'll be flexible on scheduling.

**Short version**: **True emergencies and customer impact are exceptions. Everything else respects the boundaries.**

## PHASE 11 — HOW TO HELP ME

### How to Support Me Best
1. **Bring me context, not problems.** Don't come to me with a vague "We have an issue." Come with "Here's the issue, here's what I've tried, here's what I'm recommending and why." That frame helps me help you way faster.

2. **Clear, written context.** If you're asking for my input, share a doc or a structured Slack thread. Don't expect me to gather context in a meeting.

3. **Respect my focus time.** If you have something non-urgent, wait until afternoon or send an async message. Interrupting my morning deep work is the fastest way to frustrate me.

4. **Tell me if I'm wrong.** If you disagree with a direction I'm setting, push back. I'm not always right. I'd rather hear dissent early.

5. **Assume good intent.** If I'm blunt or direct, I'm not trying to hurt you. I'm trying to be efficient. Don't take it personally.

6. **Follow up on decisions.** If I make a decision and you find new information that changes it, tell me. I don't want to defend a bad decision out of pride.

### Great Support Looks Like
From a **manager**: (1) clear strategy + autonomy to execute, (2) protection from chaos and politics, (3) honest feedback on my performance and growth, (4) advocacy for my team's resources, (5) trust that I'll deliver.

From a **peer**: (1) regular check-ins, not just when there's a problem, (2) willingness to share context early, (3) collaborative problem-solving vs. finger-pointing, (4) honesty when things aren't going well, (5) celebrating wins together.

## PHASE 12 — WORKING WITH ME

### Five Core Things to Remember
1. **I value clarity and radical honesty.** Come prepared. Say what you actually think. Don't sugarcoat.

2. **I'm direct, not mean.** Tough feedback from me is an investment in your growth, not a personal attack.

3. **I need deep focus time.** Respect my mornings. I'll give you full attention in afternoons and meetings.

4. **I follow through.** If I commit to something, it gets done. Expect the same from me.

5. **I care about my team.** I'm invested in your growth and your success. I will fight for you and clear obstacles in your way.

### First Week Onboarding
1. **Request a one-on-one.** 30 minutes, nothing formal. Tell me about yourself, your goals, what you're optimizing for.

2. **Send me a written context doc** about your role, priorities, and what you need from me. (Yes, in the first week. It helps me understand fast.)

3. **Ask three questions** about how things work here. Don't assume. Get clarity on norms.

4. **Observe a few meetings** to see how decisions get made and how I interact with my team.

5. **Offer to help with something small.** Show me you're action-oriented and collaborative. I notice people who don't just talk but do.

6. **Ask my team about me.** How do they like working with me? What surprised them? This gives you real intel fast.
